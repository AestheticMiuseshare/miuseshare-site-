Aesthetic Dissemination Exploration

Pre-reading Notes

Sentences in this text are distilled from song lyrics, built on top of the three preceding aesthetic layers:

1. Personal Artistic Perspective: Creators develop unique aesthetic awareness and creative stance, combining music, text and visuals. This is the foundation of all creation.

2. Aesthetic Architecture: Organise scattered aesthetic thoughts into a complete, stable and self-consistent system to guide creation.

3. Aesthetic Mapping: Translate creators’ inner aesthetic concepts into keywords and semantics readable by platforms, search engines and AI, bridging the gap between artworks and audiences.

Aesthetic Dissemination Exploration (Main Content)

Once creators finish the three preceding layers, works are no longer isolated outputs. Taking the music industry as an example, aesthetic dissemination uses this aesthetic framework online to bring lyrics and music to matching listeners. This method remains experimental.

This dissemination method is not a fixed template and requires continuous testing.

With solid perspective, aesthetic architecture and mapping, your work holds a unified aesthetic core. Aesthetic mapping acts as a bridge, helping recommendation systems understand the character of your lyrics and music and serve it to like-minded audiences.

1. Align Semantics for Tags and Content

Follow terms defined in aesthetic mapping to unify descriptions and tags for songs, lyrics and artwork. Keep consistent meaning across lyrics, music and visuals to reduce misunderstanding and help platforms capture your aesthetic tone.

2. Cross-platform Distribution Strategy

Audiences behave differently across platforms. Preserve your core aesthetic while creating varied content: short extracts distilled from lyric lines paired with visuals, with full audio as your primary work. Avoid copying identical full content everywhere.

3. Initial Exposure and Long-term Cultivation

The starting point of artwork dissemination is initial exposure, which begins with picking distribution channels. Independent creators have two major release options, each with distinct advantages and drawbacks.

Using an aggregate distribution service, you upload once and the work will be released globally across streaming platforms. The service handles copyright coding and royalty settlement, saving plenty of time. The downside is a cut on royalty revenue, less control over data and release settings. Also, distributors only handle publishing; they cannot guarantee listens or aesthetic resonance.

Self-release by signing up for each music platform individually grants creators full ownership, better royalty rates and direct access to playlist pitching and platform events. However, the workload is heavy. You need to register, upload and manage copyright settlement separately on every platform. You also need to handle overseas tax and platform rules by yourself, making global coverage harder.

There are two ways to gain exposure: organic algorithm distribution or paid promotion. Paid promotion buys visibility, but it cannot directly purchase aesthetic resonance from listeners. Some works gain audience recognition organically upon release. Others get only temporary clicks from ad spending without deep appreciation.

Long-term cultivation after release refers to the long-tail effect in the industry. Dissemination does not end at release. Tracks keep being discovered and retrieved over a long span of time. During this long-tail phase, creators monitor dissemination metrics and audience feedback to evaluate performance and adjust future creation or promotion plans.

Closing

This article uses practices from the music industry as a case study. This set of ideas for aesthetic dissemination can also serve as a reference for painting, literature and other art forms. As internet and AI technology keep advancing, aesthetic dissemination remains a topic worthy of ongoing exploration.
